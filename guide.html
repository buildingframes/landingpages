01 - How It Works

How cross-chain transfers
actually work

You have ETH on Ethereum. You want to use it on another network. Here is what actually happens behind the scenes - and why it involves more steps than most people expect.

"Blockchains don't natively understand each other. Moving assets between them requires infrastructure that translates between two separate systems."

Each blockchain is a self-contained ledger. Ethereum doesn't know what's happening on Arbitrum. Arbitrum doesn't know what's on Solana. They run independently, with their own rules, validators, and transaction history. When you move an asset between chains, one of several coordination models typically happens. Some systems rely on messaging layers such as CCIP or LayerZero to help coordinate communication between networks:

01

Lock and mint

Your asset is locked on the origin chain. A representation - a "wrapped" version - is created on the destination chain. You end up with a synthetic token that represents your original asset, not the original itself.

02

Burn and mint

Your asset is destroyed on the origin chain and recreated on the destination chain. Cleaner, but relies on the bridge protocol having the right infrastructure in place on both networks.

03

Liquidity pools

Some bridges hold pools of assets on each chain and swap between them. You send ETH on one chain, the bridge sends ETH from its pool on another. Fast, but dependent on available liquidity.

The key point

In all three cases, a third-party protocol handles coordination between chains. That intermediary layer is where most of the complexity - and most of the risk - lives.

02 - The Friction

Why it's still more complicated
than it should be

There are now hundreds of active blockchain networks. The multichain ecosystem is growing fast. But the infrastructure connecting these networks has not kept up. Here is why the experience is still fragmented.

The silo problem

Each network builds for itself

Wallets, tools, and interfaces are built for individual networks. The person using three chains at once is an afterthought, not the primary user.

The context problem

Every switch means starting over

Different interfaces, different mental models, different fee structures. Moving between networks means rebuilding context every time.

Hundreds

Active blockchain networks operating across fragmented ecosystems

$1.1B+

Lost to bridge-related exploits - a direct consequence of custody architecture

"The problem is not a lack of good networks. It is that those networks were not designed to work together - and the infrastructure to connect them is still catching up."

This is not a criticism of any individual network. Each chain optimises for its own users and ecosystem. Cross-chain coordination simply was not the primary design goal. The result is a landscape that is powerful in parts but fragmented as a whole - felt most by the people navigating across it every day.

03 - Before You Bridge

What to know before
moving assets across networks

Moving assets between blockchains is now common. But it carries real considerations worth understanding before you start. Here is a practical checklist.

Educational Disclaimer

This guide is for educational purposes only and should not be considered financial advice or an endorsement of any specific protocol, bridge, or digital asset.

Understand what you're actually receiving. In many cross-chain transfers, what arrives may be a bridged, wrapped, or represented version of the original asset. Check whether the destination token is native, bridged, or otherwise represented across networks.

Account for the full cost. Bridge fees, gas on origin chain, gas on destination chain, and potential slippage can add up significantly. Calculate the total cost before initiating, not after.

Verify the bridge protocol you are using. Not all bridges carry the same risk profile. The custody architecture determines the security model. Research before you use.

Have gas on the destination chain. A common mistake is arriving on a new chain with the bridged asset but no native token to pay gas. Ensure you have gas on the destination before initiating.

Allow for confirmation time. Cross-chain transfers are not instant. Depending on the networks and bridge involved, transfers can take minutes to hours.

Test with a small amount first. Before moving significant value across an unfamiliar bridge or new network, test the route with a small amount to confirm everything works as expected.

The bigger picture

These considerations exist because blockchain networks still operate independently, with fragmented coordination between ecosystems. Frame is a blockchain designed to help connect fragmented networks and simplify the multichain experience over time.

04 - What Simpler Looks Like

What simpler multichain
infrastructure looks like

The friction of moving between blockchain networks is not inevitable. It is a consequence of infrastructure that was not designed with interoperability as a first principle. Here is what the infrastructure layer needs to do differently.

"The complexity of cross-chain coordination should be absorbed at the infrastructure layer - not passed to the developer or the user."

01

Networks that communicate natively

Simpler multichain infrastructure starts with networks designed to work together, not in isolation. Interoperability as a first principle, not an afterthought bolted on later.

02

No custody of assets

The security risk of current bridge architecture comes from concentrating assets in custodial pools. Better infrastructure coordinates between chains without holding user assets in between.

03

Consistent experience across networks

Users should not need to rebuild context every time they move between chains. The interface, mental model, and fee structure should feel consistent regardless of which network is underneath.

04

Infrastructure that benefits from all networks

Better infrastructure is aligned differently - it grows when cross-chain activity grows, creating the right incentive to make interoperability work.
